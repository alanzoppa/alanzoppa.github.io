History of a Megalopolis
1325
Tenochtitlán Founded
The Mexica people, guided by the prophecy of an eagle on a cactus, built their city on an island in Lake Texcoco. By 1519, it was one of the largest cities on Earth — with canals, floating gardens, and a population of over 200,000.
1521
Spanish Conquest
Hernán Cortés and his indigenous allies besieged Tenochtitlán for 93 days. The city fell, and from its stones rose the colonial capital of New Spain — the cathedral built atop the Templo Mayor.
1810–1821
Independence
Miguel Hidalgo's cry for independence — "El Grito de Dolores" — ignited an 11-year struggle. Mexico emerged as a sovereign nation, and the capital became the seat of a new republic.
1950–Today
Modern Megalopolis
Post-war boom turned the city into a global cultural powerhouse. Today, CDMX is the largest city in North America — a sprawling, chaotic, electrifying metropolis that never stops reinventing itself.
Culture & the Arts
Muralism
Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros painted Mexico's history onto public walls. Their monumental works at Palacio Nacional and Bellas Artes defined a national visual identity — art for the people, not the gallery.
Frida Kahlo
Pain, passion, and fierce individuality. Frida's Casa Azul in Coyoacán is now a pilgrimage site. Her unflinching self-portraits blended Mexican folk art with surrealism and feminist defiance — an icon whose power only grows with time.
Día de Muertos
Every November 1-2, the city erupts in marigolds, sugar skulls, and ofrendas. The dead return — and the living celebrate. Parades wind through Centro Histórico; families gather in candlelit cemeteries. It is joy, memory, and resistance.
Mariachi
Born in Jalisco, perfected in Garibaldi Plaza. Trumpets, violins, and gut-wrenching vocals fill the night air as bands compete for your attention. A UNESCO Intangible Heritage — and the soundtrack of every Mexican celebration.
Son Jarocho
From Veracruz to the capital, this Afro-Mexican tradition pulses with zapateado footwork and improvised verse. The jarana and requinto drive rhythms that have endured for centuries — raw, communal, and utterly alive.
Lucha Libre
Masked heroes and villains collide in a theater of flying bodies and moral drama. Arena México is the cathedral — Tuesday and Friday nights, the rudos and técnicos put on a show that is part sport, part soap opera, and pure México.
Colonias — The Soul of the City
Roma
Bohemian & Hip
Tree-lined streets, Art Nouveau mansions, and the best coffee in Latin America. Roma Norte is where filmmakers, designers, and chefs collide — a neighborhood that feels like a Wes Anderson set with a mezcal buzz.
Condesa
Leafy & Elegant
Parque México anchors this art deco paradise. Dog walkers, joggers, and weekend brunchers fill its paths. Condesa is Roma's quieter, greener sister — effortlessly chic and endlessly strollable.
Coyoacán
Village Charm
Cobblestone streets, colonial churches, and Frida's blue house. Once a separate village, Coyoacán still feels apart — artists sell their work in the plaza, churros steam in the afternoon sun, and time slows to a colonial pace.
Centro Histórico
Monumental & Ancient
The Zócalo — one of the world's largest public squares — is the beating heart. Aztec ruins lie beneath Spanish palaces. Every street corner holds five centuries of layered history, street commerce, and relentless energy.
Polanco
Polished & Global
Designer boutiques, embassies, and Michelin-starred restaurants line Avenida Masaryk — Mexico's Champs-Élysées. Polanco is sleek, cosmopolitan, and home to the stunning Museo Soumaya with its free Rodin collection.
San Ángel
Artistic & Colonial
Saturday's Bazar del Sábado fills cobbled plazas with paintings and silver. The cobblestone streets, flower-filled courtyards, and Diego Rivera's twin-house studio make San Ángel feel suspended in a more beautiful century.
The Food Capital of the Americas
Tacos al Pastor
Lebanese immigrants brought the shawarma; Mexicans added pork, achiote, and pineapple. The trompo spins on every corner — slice, char, serve on a tiny tortilla with salsa verde. Perfection costs 15 pesos.
Street Corn & Tlacoyos
Elotes slathered in mayo, chili, and cotija cheese. Blue-corn tlacoyos stuffed with fava beans and topped with nopales. The street is the city's true kitchen — and the best meals come on a paper plate.
Mercado de San Juan
The chef's market. Exotic produce, wild mushrooms, and every part of every animal. International chefs shop here alongside grandmothers — and the seafood counter serves ceviche that rivals the city's best restaurants.
La Merced
The largest retail market in Latin America. A labyrinth of spices, moles, dried chiles, and fresh masa. The energy is overwhelming, the scale is unfathomable, and the smells will follow you home in the best way.
Pujol & Quintonil
Two of the World's 50 Best Restaurants. Enrique Olvera's Pujol serves a mole that aged 1,500 days. Jorge Vallejo's Quintonil elevates chapulines and hoja santa to high art. CDMX is a fine-dining supernova.
Mezcal & Pulque
Smoky agave spirits from Oaxaca flow freely in Roma's mezcalerías. And pulque — the ancient fermented sap of the maguey, once reserved for Aztec priests — is having a revival among the young and curious.

Must-Visit Icons
01
Zócalo & Templo Mayor
One of the largest public plazas on Earth. Beneath it: the Aztec Templo Mayor, where archaeologists still unearth offerings to Huitzilopochtli. The cathedral and National Palace frame the square in colonial grandeur.
02
Teotihuacán Pyramids
An hour from the city, the Pyramid of the Sun and Moon rise from the Avenue of the Dead. Built by a civilization that vanished before the Aztecs arrived. Climb them at sunrise — it is unforgettable.
03
Xochimilco Canals
The last remnants of the Aztec lake system. Board a colorful trajinera, hire a mariachi band, and float past chinampas — ancient floating gardens still farmed today. Pure, chaotic, floating joy.
04
Chapultepec Park
Twice the size of Central Park. Museums, lakes, botanical gardens, and the castle. The Museo Nacional de Antropología here is arguably the greatest museum in the Americas — the Aztec Sun Stone alone is worth the trip.
05
Casa Azul
Frida Kahlo's cobalt-blue childhood home in Coyoacán, where she lived with Diego Rivera and died. Her easel, her corsets, her bed — everything remains. Tickets sell out weeks ahead. Book early.
06
Arena México
The cathedral of Lucha Libre. Tuesday nights are for rookies; Fridays for the stars. Buy a mask outside, drink a michelada, and boo the rudos with 16,000 screaming fans. No translation required.
Practical Know-How
Best Time
November to April: dry, mild days, cool nights. The jacarandas bloom purple in March. Avoid late summer — afternoon downpours are biblical, and the city's drainage is, shall we say, historic.
Altitude
2,250m (7,380 ft) — higher than Denver. Your first day: hydrate, skip the mezcal, walk slowly. Your lungs adapt by day two, but that initial headache is real.
Getting Around
Metro: 5 pesos, one of the world's cheapest. Metrobús for cross-town. Uber is cheap and everywhere. The airport is 20 minutes from Roma — no excuse to linger in your hotel.
Stay Smart
CDMX is safer than its reputation. Stick to well-trafficked colonias at night, use Uber after dark, and keep your phone tucked away on the Metro. Common sense goes far here.
